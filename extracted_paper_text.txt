An intelligent healthcare system for rare disease 
diagnosis utilizing electronic health records 
based on a knowledge-guided multimodal 
transformer framework
Ahed Abugabah1 , Prashant Kumar Shukla2, Piyush Kumar Shukla3 and Ankur Pandey4*
BioData Mining
Abstract
Rare diseases are a common problem with millions of patients globally, but their 
diagnosis is difficult because of varied clinical presentations, small sample size, and 
disparate biomedical data sources. Current diagnostic tools are not able to combine 
multimodal information effectively, which results in a timely or wrong diagnosis. To 
fill this gap, this paper suggests a smart multimodal healthcare framework integrating 
electronic health records (EHRs), genomic sequences, and medical imaging to 
improve the detection of rare diseases. The framework uses Swin Transformer to 
extract hierarchical visual features in radiographic scans, Med-BERT and Transformer-
XL to learn semantic and long-term temporal relations in longitudinal electronic 
health record narratives, and a Graph Neural Network (GNN)-based encoder to learn 
functional and structural relations in genomic sequences. The alignment of the 
cross-modal representation is further boosted with a Knowledge-Guided Contrastive 
Learning (KGCL) mechanism, which takes advantage of rare disease ontologies in 
Orphanet to improve the interpretability of the model and infusion of knowledge. 
To achieve strong performance, the Nutcracker Optimization Algorithm (NOA) is 
proposed to optimize hyperparameters, calibrate attention mechanisms, and enhance 
multimodal fusion. Experimental results on MIMIC-IV (EHR), ClinVar (genomics), 
and CheXpert (imaging) datasets show that the proposed framework significantly 
outperforms the state-of-the-art multimodal baselines in terms of accuracy and 
robustness of early rare disease diagnosis. This paper presents the opportunity to 
integrate hierarchical vision transformers, domain-specific language models, graph-
based genomic encoders, and knowledge-directed optimization to make explainable, 
accurate, and clinically applicable healthcare decisions in rare disease settings.
Keywords Rare disease diagnosis, Multimodal healthcare framework, Swin 
transformer, Graph neural networks (GNNs), Knowledge-Guided contrastive learning 
(KGCL), Nutcracker optimization algorithm (NOA)

Introduction
Rare diseases are characterized by the fact that they affect a tiny proportion of the popu -
lation, and this aspect alone affects over 300 million people globally [ 1]. This massive 
burden notwithstanding, the diagnosis of rare diseases is a protracted and unpredictable 
process, commonly described as a diagnostic odyssey [ 2]. Patients, on average, spend 
years of misdiagnosis and untimely treatment, mostly because of the lack of homogene -
ity of clinical manifestations, the lack of large datasets, and biomedical evidence frag -
mentation [3]. Conventional tests, which depend on the expertise of the physicians and 
use of single modalities, often fail to detect rare conditions early and accurately.
The introduction of Electronic Health Records (EHRs) at scale, genomic sequencing, 
and medical imaging modalities has presented the opportunity to expedite the process of 
detecting rare diseases like never before. EHRs provide longitudinal phenotypic trends, 
genomics has the potential to identify the causative genetic variants, and imaging can 
demonstrate structural or functional abnormalities that are essential to diagnosis [ 4–6]. 
However, these modalities are usually addressed separately in current diagnostic pipe -
lines. The lack of effective integration of multimodal data is also a major impediment 
that causes incomplete representations and less-than-optimal clinical decision-making 
[7].
Recent breakthroughs in deep learning have motivated the effort to fill this gap by 
providing automated representation learning over a variety of biomedical data. CNNs 
have long been the mainstay of medical image analysis, but can have difficulty with long-
range dependency modeling and multimodal integration scalability [ 8]. Transformer 
architectures have emerged as better alternatives, since these models have self-attention 
mechanisms, which are good at learning global contextual relationships in both sequen -
tial and visual data [9]. Transformers are more adaptable, have better feature representa-
tion, and better ability to incorporate heterogeneous data, which makes them especially 
suitable for multimodal healthcare applications compared to CNNs [ 10]. This strength 
is emphasized in the recent work comparing transformer-based and CNN-based mod -
els on complex biomedical tasks [ 11]. Moreover, recent developments in explainable AI 
(XAI) in medical imaging have highlighted that transformer-based models not only have 
better accuracy but also have better interpretability, which is essential to clinical accep -
tance [12].
Although these are positive trends, there still exist some gaps that have not been filled. 
Current multimodal models do not usually take advantage of domain-specific knowl -
edge sources, such as rare disease ontologies, which can be used to inform model inter -
pretability and robustness. Second, several methods do not have effective optimization 
strategies for hyperparameters and attention calibration, which restricts their scalability 
to a wide range of patients. Lastly, integration frameworks are often built with perfor -
mance in mind rather than explainability, and this limits their applicability in real-world 
clinical practice.
This paper presents a multimodal healthcare system that considers EHRs, genomic 
data, and medical images in diagnosing rare diseases to overcome these challenges. 
The framework uses Swin Transformer to obtain hierarchical representations of imag -
ing data, Med-BERT and Transformer-XL to model EHRs semantically and temporally, 
and a Graph Neural Network (GNN) to model genomic relations. A Knowledge-Guided 
Contrastive Learning (KGCL) mechanism uses the Orphanet ontologies to make it more

Literature review
Diagnosis of rare diseases has been addressed in a variety of ways, spanning interdisci -
plinary clinical methods to the newest deep learning models. Lillig et al. [ 13] reviewed 
the impact of interdisciplinary case conferences and exome sequencing in the Translate-
NAMSE project, where one-third of patients obtained a definite diagnosis and one-
third of exome analyses resulted in genetic findings. The authors noted, though, that an 
expanded use of whole-genome sequencing and global registries would be required to 
increase the coverage of diagnosis.
Transformer-based architecture has attracted attention to multimodal healthcare 
tasks with the popularity of deep learning. Zhou et al. [ 14] proposed a unified trans -
former framework, which is a combination of early and late fusion and attention 
mechanism applied across image data, which demonstrated a significant increase in 
predicting COVID-19 outcomes and pulmonary diseases compared with image-only 
models. Although it was successful, the study was hampered by small and homogeneous 
datasets. Likewise, Jordan et al. [ 15] showed RarePT, a transformer-based model trained 
on EHR diagnosis codes, which indicated that in large health systems, more than half 
of the patients were flagged as potentially undiagnosed in 20 of 32 diseases. However, 
its usefulness was limited by the absence of a straightforward way of validating undiag -
nosed cases.
Dwelling upon the problem of recognizing not only known but also unknown diseases, 
Luo and Yuan [16] have offered a Semantic-guided Rare Disease Diagnosis (SRDD) sys -
tem, which includes saliency discovery and synthetic sampling strategies. Their approach 
achieved state-of-the-art classification and was able to identify previously unknown dis -
eases, albeit it was sensitive to data scarcity. In a similar way, Du and Xingwu [ 17] took 
advantage of uncertainty in chest X-ray diagnostics by combining the CNN-extracted 
visual features with a transformer model in label associations. Their framework

performed better than models with fixed labels, but was impaired by the ambiguity of 
labels in multi-disease data.
Other machine learning based methodologies have also made significant contributions 
in rare disease research. Bahadure et al. [ 18] used classic machine learning methods to 
predict the severity of rare diseases based on patient records and demonstrated that they 
have improved in the areas of diagnostic support, but their performance was limited by 
the small and potentially biased data. In another, larger-scale data-driven study, Moyni -
han et al. [ 19] used EHRs of more than 1.28 million patients and were able to identify 
new Fabry disease cases that were undiagnosed as well as several thousand individuals 
who could be considered to have familial hypercholesterolemia. Nevertheless, despite 
such findings, there is still a challenge of EHR data mining in terms of data quality and 
validation. Similarly, Dong et al. [ 20] proposed a weakly supervised, ontology-guided 
framework of rare disease identification using clinical notes, with a precision of concept 
linking that is improved by up to 50%, due to imperfect mapping between ontologies 
(UMLS and ORDO); there was a lack of generalizability.
Transformer-based multimodal learning has received more and more attention in 
recent works. Li et al. [ 21] suggested a multimodal transformer with longitudinal inte -
gration of imaging data with clinical signatures to classify pulmonary nodules, which 
showed significant increases in AUC, but had issues with irregularly sampled modalities. 
Niu et al. [22] moved this field forward with EHR-KnowGen, which used large language 
models and soft prompts to achieve unified multimodal learning and recorded better 
performance in diagnosis generation, but multimodal integration problems remained. 
In a similar vein, Chen et al. [ 23] proposed a disentangle-and-calibrate framework with 
gradient guidance, which performed state-of-the-art on common and rare disease diag -
nosis, but required large, labeled datasets.
Complementary views are provided by systematic reviews and expert guidelines. 
According to Gbole and Asiamah [ 24], CNNs have demonstrated high accuracy in 
rare disease imaging, yet they lack proper generalizability because they use small and 
closed datasets with no external validation. Van Karnebeek et al. [ 25] highlighted the 
promise of genomics, epigenomics, and multi-omics technologies and the importance 
of global collaboration and the equitable access of diagnostic innovations to patients, 
noting that more than half of rare diseases remain undiagnosed. Lastly, PhenoBrain [ 26] 
is an NLP pipeline that uses BERT to extract phenotypes in EHRs and is more accurate 
than human experts in differentiating rare diseases with a top-3 recall of 0.613. However, 
its analysis was in a simulated workflow and not a real-world workflow, and the lack of 
architectural transparency compromised reproducibility. Table 1 provides an overview 
of the literature review.
Collectively, the literature shows that there is considerable advancement in EHR 
mining, genomic sequencing, medical imaging, and transformer-based multimodal 
fusion to diagnose rare diseases. Nevertheless, there are severe gaps. To begin with, the 
majority of frameworks continue to treat data sources in silos or use minimal integra -
tion strategies, which limit diagnostic robustness. Second, previous studies seldom use 
domain knowledge that is available in rare disease ontologies, and this domain knowl -
edge may increase the interpretation and clinical credibility. Third, hyperparameter and 
attention-mechanism optimization in multimodal fusion is an under-explored area,

EHR narratives
Med-BERT architecture In this work, the Med-BERT [ 28] architecture was used to 
process structured electronic health records (EHRs) as an extension of the transformer-
based design of the original BERT model. In contrast to BERT, which works on a one-
dimensional sequence of words, Med-BERT processes the EHR as a sequence of patient 
visits that are represented by diagnosis codes. Med-BERT combines three different 
embedding layers to capture the semantics, the sequential, and the temporal embedding 
of the data in order to achieve effective representation. Diagnosis codes are mapped into 
low-dimensional vector representations to represent clinical entities, serialization rep -
resentations are integrated to capture the relative order and contextual priority of codes 
within a single visit, and visit representations introduce temporal distinctions across mul-
tiple encounters. The model integrates these embeddings to produce representations of 
input that are processed by bidirectional transformer encoders, which allows the model 
to identify complex correlations within multilayered and multi-relational EHR structures. 
Med-BERT achieves this by creating a deep contextual representation of patient histories 
through pretraining techniques, including masked code prediction and visit classifica -
tion, resulting in strong downstream clinical prediction performance.

Nutcracker optimization algorithm

the foraging, caching, and recovery behavior of nutcrackers in the wild. More specifi -
cally, NOA resembles the way in which nutcrackers gather seeds in warmer seasons, hide 
them in several caches, and then reclaim them in the winter and spring. These biological 
mechanisms are codified into two major strategies: foraging and storage (exploration-
exploitation tradeoff) and cache-search and recovery (memory-guided optimization).

Datasets
MIMIC-IV
The MIMIC-IV [ 33] is a large-scale, de-identified database that offers comprehensive 
electronic health records (EHRs) of the patients who were admitted to the Beth Israel 
Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, Massachusetts. In particular, it contains the data 
of more than 65,000 patients who have been admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) 
and more than 200,000 patients who have been admitted to the emergency department. 
MIMIC-IV builds upon MIMIC-III, but includes more modern data and a modular 
design to improve data provenance and enable independent and integrated use of het -
erogeneous data. It includes structured clinical data, physician notes, lab reports, and 
medication histories, providing a longitudinal view of patient trajectories. This tempo -
ral richness renders it especially appropriate to the examination of disease progression, 
response to treatment, and outcome prediction, which are key areas in the detection of 
rare diseases.
MIMIC-IV is a rich source of demographic, clinical, and outcome data that will allow 
multimodal analysis when paired with imaging, genomic, and ontological data. Table  2 
shows the demographics and outcome summary of ICU patients in MIMIC-IV. The sta -
tistics reflect the heterogeneity of patient populations, such as age distribution, gender 
representation, insurance coverage, length of stay, and in-hospital and one-year mortal -
ity rates. Such extensive demographic coverage will allow predictive models trained on

ClinVar
ClinVar [34] is a free, publicly accessible resource that gives detailed information about 
human genetic variations, their categories, and their relationships with diseases and 
drug responses. It also has a key role to play in connecting the worlds of researchers, 
clinicians, and the medical genetics community at large by providing raw submissions 
and aggregate insights. The archive will accept submissions at a variety of levels of detail, 
with some basic variant-level records containing little or no supporting evidence, and 
others at the case-level or functional dataset level. A given submission generally contains 
the description of the genetic variant, its clinical classification, association with a disease 
or drug response, and the literature or experimental data supporting the submission.
To make genomic studies consistent and interoperable, ClinVar adheres to the HGVS 
standard of variant reporting and aligns all submissions to reference sequences. Its pecu-
liarity is the accessioning system, which systematizes and traces the information effi -
ciently in three-level hierarchies. Records submitted are assigned an SCV (Submitted 
Record) number, and related submissions that describe the same variant-condition pair 
are placed under an RCV (Reference Accession). All records of the same genetic variant, 
irrespective of condition, are in turn aggregated into a VCV (Variation Accession). The 
layered structure not only facilitates efficient organization but also enables transparency 
and re-evaluation with time since there is a history of the versions. Table  3 shows the 
overview of clinvar dataset metrics.
Another important feature of ClinVar is its review status, which explains the level of 
consensus on which variant was classified and whether there are conflicting interpreta -
tions of the variant or whether it has been reviewed by a panel of experts. This aids in the 
determination of the reliability and clinical significance of the data by the user. ClinVar 
also works closely with other such initiatives as ClinGen, which reinforces its position as 
a source of evidence-based variant interpretation and standardization in clinical genom -
ics. By providing a place to accommodate both agreement and contradictory statements, 
ClinVar will enable refinement of genomic knowledge over time and help achieve preci -
sion medicine.

CheXpert
CheXpert [35] is one of the largest publicly available chest X-ray datasets that is designed 
to advance automated medical image interpretation. It comprises 224,316 chest radio -
graphs of 65,240 patients that were taken at Stanford Health Care between October 2002 
and July 2017, both inpatient and outpatient.
All images are matched with their respective radiology report, allowing visual and tex -
tual access to clinical research. CheXpert has a unique selling point in that it includes 
uncertainty labels that reflect the uncertainty that is usually associated with radiological 
evaluations. These labels enable training not only on positive and negative results but 
also on cases where the diagnosis is uncertain–bringing machine learning nearer to the 
real world of clinical decision-making.
CheXpert has annotations on 14 prevalent pathologies of the thorax, including pneu -
monia, atelectasis, pleural effusion, cardiomegaly, and pneumothorax. A rule-based nat -
ural language processing (NLP) pipeline was created to automate the labeling process, 
which was validated against radiologist consensus. The dataset also has a reference stan -
dard evaluation set with expert interpretations by a radiologist, allowing the comparison 
of models with expert interpretations. Figure  5 shows the chest x-ray images from 65k 
patients in frontal and lateral views.
Orphanet ontology
Orphanet [36] is a multinational comprehensive knowledge base specializing in rare dis -
eases and orphan drugs, maintained by a European consortium of experts. Its ontology 
gives a hierarchical classification scheme of over 6,000 rare diseases, with their pheno -

to general biomedical ontologies, Orphanet specifically addresses uncommon and rare 
diseases, and thus is especially valuable to this study. The distribution of disorders by

etiological subtype and morphological anomaly, occurring less frequently. This imbal -
ance creates bias during the training of the models as the less represented categories are 
highly likely to be misclassified, thus constraining generalizations.

Preprocessing
Text normalization
EHRs and radiology reports were converted to textual data and were rigorously normal -
ized to achieve semantic consistency in heterogeneous clinical narratives. UMLS and 
SNOMED CT [ 37] were used as standardized biomedical terminologies to normalize 
the medical concepts and lessen the variability of documentation. We removed non-
informative tokens, repetitive metadata, and generic stop words. More emphasis was put 
on negation detection (e.g., pneumonia vs. no pneumonia) in order to maintain clini -
cal meaning. The resulting text was tokenized into sentences and then tokenized at a 
subword level so that it can be used with transformer-based models like MedBERT and 
Transformer-XL that need structured sequential input.

The preprocessing of chest radiographs obtained in the CheXpert dataset standardized 
the visual inputs to meet the needs of deep learning. All images were resized to 224 224

Genomic graph construction
Genomic data preprocessing concentrated on building heterogeneous graph representa -
tions to represent the complexity of gene-variant-disease relationships. Variant-disease 
associations were obtained using ClinVar, protein-protein interaction (PPI) data were 
obtained using STRING, and regulatory pathway data were obtained using RegulonDB. 
These data were integrated into a single graph corresponding to a set of nodes represent-
ing a gene, a variant, or a disease, and edges between any two nodes that represented 
biological or functional relationships. All of the variant nodes were further augmented 
with attributes such as allele frequency, pathogenicity scores, and functional annota -
tions, resulting in a biologically informed feature space. This multimodal graph repre -
sentation facilitated downstream learning with GNN encoders to enable the discovery of 
complex genetic signatures in rare diseases.
Training configurations
All modality-specific encoders were trained with standard configuration, and the unified 
fusion framework was also trained with standardized settings to enable fair compari -
sons across experiments. All the models were trained in PyTorch 2.0 with CUDA accel -
eration on NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPUs with 12GB memory. Transformer-based modules, 
such as the Swin Transformer, Med-BERT, and Transformer-XL, were pretrained with 
biomedical weights, and the genomic sequence graph encoder was initialized according 
to Xavier initialization. Parameters were updated with the AdamW optimizer that adds 
weight decay to increase stability and reduce overfitting.
Hyperparameter optimization was performed with the NOA rather than using manual 
tuning or grid search [ 39]. Based on a nature-inspired metaheuristic, NOA is adaptively 
able to balance the exploration of broad hyperparameter ranges with the exploitation 
of the most promising hyperparameter settings. NOA optimized over a set of impor -
tant hyperparameters, including the learning rate, dropout probability, weight decay, 
and batch size, iteratively refining the candidate solutions. The training process has con -
verted to a learning rate of about 2.3 × 10−4, dropout probability of 0.25, weight decay 
factor adjusted in the range of 10−6 to 10−3, and a batch size of 64. These parameters 
were identified as maximizing the performance of validation across tasks.
The training was carried out over 100 epochs with cosine annealing of the learning 
rate and linear warm-up over the first ten epochs to stabilize convergence. In every 
training batch, 64 multimodal samples were used, and gradient accumulation was used 
to address the GPU memory limitation. Dropout and label smoothing were used to sup -
port regularization, which helped to generalize across modalities. Loss functions were 
task-specific, where weighted cross-entropy was used on EHR and imaging classifica -
tion, graph-based contrastive loss on node classification on genomic learning, and a

knowledge-guided contrastive objective incorporated into the multimodal fusion stage. 
Early stopping with patience set to fifteen epochs was used to prevent overfitting, and 
the best-performing model checkpoints were saved to be used in the final evaluation.

Temporal convergence and stability
We also investigated the convergence property of training of different fusion strategies 
to measure optimization stability. The standard deviation of the loss change (DeltaLt) 
over 50 epochs was adopted as a measure of stability, with lower values indicating a 
smoother, reliable convergence. The epochs it took to converge were also noted. Table  9

tent gradient updates than Early Fusion (0.015) and Late Fusion (0.012). It also converges 
in only 28 epochs, which is roughly 30% less than the 41 epochs taken by Early Fusion. 
These results indicate that the knowledge-assisted contrastive alignment not only 
improves accuracy, as shown in Sect. 5.2, but also speeds up convergence and minimizes 
training instability.
Generalization ability
To assess the degree of generalization of the models beyond the data used in training, we 
calculated the generalization gap (G), which is defined as the difference between the val -
idation loss and the training loss. The narrower the gap, the more robust and less overfit.

The most important insights are that the Proposed Framework reaches the lowest 
training and validation loss, which means the model fits better but does not overfit. It 
has a significantly lower generalization gap at 0.037 compared to Early Fusion (0.079) 
and Late Fusion (0.067), indicating that its combination with multimodal fusion not only 
leads to an increase in accuracy but also enhances the capability of the framework to

Dataset limitations and generalization
One of the main challenges that are faced in this study is the bias in the dataset that influ-
ences the generalizability of the proposed framework. As an example, MIMIC-IV is mostly 
used in North American hospitals, which lowers its usability in other healthcare systems that 
have different clinical practices and patient demographics. Likewise, CheXpert is skewed 
to adult populations, with few pediatric imaging cases, and ClinVar is skewed to European 
ancestries, with few other populations. These are the limitations that inhibit the ability of 
the framework to perform uniformly across demographics and clinical settings. To reduce 
these problems, we used data augmentation, transfer learning, and ontology-guided learning 
(KGCL) to enhance robustness and fairness. However, a lack of multi-center external valida-
tion still remains a limitation, as it was raised by Reviewer 3. Future work will be needed to 
overcome this limitation by including multi-center, demographically-representative datasets 
to improve fairness, generalizability, and clinical reliability.
Practical and clinical implications
Clinically, the framework proposed can have great applicability to real-life practice. It com-
bines EHR, imaging, and genomic data to improve early diagnosis of complex and rare dis-
eases with a more comprehensive and timely view for the physician. The inclusion of KGCL 
enhances explainability, and it is easier to understand how predictions are made based on 
ontology-based reasoning, thus creating more confidence and openness in clinical decision-
making. Significantly, the framework minimizes the chances of misdiagnosis, especially in 
cases where the symptoms are overlapping or rare, and using a single modality is usually 
ineffective. Of equal importance is its feasibility of deployment: the scalable architectures 
based on transformers make it compatible with the current hospital IT infrastructure, and 
therefore, the adoption barrier is minimal. In providing these points, the framework directly 
answers the challenge posed by Reviewer 1 to provide real-world applicability and its signifi-
cance as a decision-support tool towards precision medicine development.
Conclusion
The proposed study is based on a brand-new KGCL multimodal framework that can be used 
to diagnose rare diseases early with the assistance of EHRs, genomic sequences, and medical 
imaging. The proposed framework uses Swin Transformer to extract visual features hierar-
chically, Med-BERT and Transformer-XL to model the EHRs semantically and temporally, 
and GNN to capture the functional and structural relationships in genomics. The alignment 
across modalities is further augmented by KGCL, which uses domain knowledge of rare dis-
ease ontologies, and hyperparameters and attention mechanisms are optimized by NOA.
